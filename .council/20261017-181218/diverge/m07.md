## Baseline (the obvious answers)
- Caption each shot with a small local VLM so `pic` lines say who and what is in the frame, not "medium gray area at bottom-left".
- Bind voice letters to faces (on screen or voice-over), so the agent knows which person in a tile is speaking.
- Put vocal events (laughter, sighs, applause) and loudness on the speech lines they overlap.

## Ideas
### m07.1 Subtitles for the deaf (SDH) as the free, timed judge for sound
The bench cannot hold a sound question because its key writer cannot hear. Human captioners have already solved this. SDH and CC tracks on films, TV uploads and many YouTube channels mark timed bracket cues such as `[door slams]`, `[sighs]`, `[laughter]`, `(off-screen)`, `♪ tense music ♪` and `[speaking Vietnamese]`. Those cues are both a key for sound lines on real footage and a census, made by professionals, of what a viewer who cannot hear needs written down. They show which sounds are worth a line and which are texture. Cheapest settling observation: `yt-dlp --list-subs` plus a subtitle download on about 30 candidate videos, then count bracket cues per minute. With 5 or more videos at 2 or more cues per minute, a real-footage sound bench exists for free (score: share of cues within 1.5 s of a `snd` line whose name matches). With fewer, the idea dies in an afternoon.

### m07.2 Persistent on-screen text never reaches the card
`_ocr_events` OCRs only the before and after crops of *changed* regions and reports their diff. Text that is on screen from the first frame and never changes, or that stays the same across a cut, is therefore never read. Examples are a TikTok headline banner ("Theo WHO: 1/3 ..."), a lower-third name or a slide title the speaker stands in front of. In a fact-check that text is often the claim itself. The fix is to OCR the full frame once per shot (the shot-first frames are already extracted for flow sheets), then print text that lasts at least 5 s once as `on screen 00:00-00:45: "..."`. Cheapest settling observation: run Vision OCR on the flow-sheet tiles already cached for the ~7 real cards, and count strings that appear in at least half the tiles but never in the card. Zero means drop the idea. Any hit is a measured blind spot with a ready-made test.

### m07.3 Stop erasing the synthetic voice, and stop calling a song a statement
`see.py` maps the AudioSet class "Speech synthesizer" to the plain word "speech" (`ALIAS`, line 416), so the card deletes the one signal that says a narration is TTS. CapCut-style TTS voices are common on Vietnamese TikTok. For a fact-check, a synthetic narrator over stock footage is a different witness from a person on camera. In the same way, a `say` line over a run CED hears as Singing is a lyric, not a claim. The fix is a footer word per voice letter (`voice A: synthetic`), and `sing` instead of `say` where Singing beats Speech over the line. Both use scores the tagger already computes, so the running cost is zero. Cheapest settling observation: take CED's mean Speech-synthesizer score per line on 10 TTS clips (macOS `say`, a Vietnamese TTS, real CapCut reels) and 10 human reels. Ship only if one threshold separates them with no false "synthetic" on the human set.

### m07.4 Make the card survive `| head` and `| tail`
In 2 of about 7 real sessions the agent truncated the card itself. The voice legend that defines the letters sits at the very bottom, the timeline is chronological, and so `head -120` silently drops the end of the video and the meaning of every `[voice C]`. A sense the agent cuts off is a sense it does not have. The fix is to order the card for truncation. The first ~15 lines carry the legend, persistent text, counts per sense and the densest spans. When the card is longer than N lines, `see` prints that head plus the path to the full `card.md`, so a pipe can no longer lose data without saying so. Cheapest settling observation: grep all session transcripts for `sift see .*\| *(head|tail)` to get the true rate. Then rerun the real-footage bench with every card cut at 120 lines, old order against new order.

### m07.5 Edit seams inside speech: where a quote was cut
A clip can make someone "say" something by cutting out the question before it or the clause after it, and the agent cannot hear the jump. A seam is a picture cut that lands inside or at the edge of a speech line, where the background (CED's non-speech class profile and the noise floor in the pauses) jumps and the sentence is incomplete across it. Mark it on that `say` line as `| edit here`. The roadmap already holds a real case: clip `tDmjz6HB-yw` is cut from a 47-minute talk and drops the interviewer's question. Cheapest settling observation: both files are cached, so align their transcripts to list the true seams. Check that the rule fires on them and nowhere else in the source talk. Then splice 20 synthetic seams into LibriSpeech dialogues to get precision and recall.

### m07.6 Audit the one miss before building a sense for it
The only shared real-footage miss (phone reel q06, a gesture at a spoken phrase) is the obvious target for a new "gesture" sense. But its key was written from 2 fps sheets, so the key's own timing is uncertain by about 0.5 s, about as wide as the event in question. The miss may be a key error. Cheapest settling observation: open 15 fps frames over the phrase (word times from the ASR) and re-key q06 by hand, which takes ten minutes. Make it a rule as well: every bench question records the time resolution its answer needs, and any question finer than its key's resolution is flagged as unscorable rather than counted as a miss.

### m07.7 Mine the real jobs, not the zoom log, for the gap list
The real sessions ask questions the bench never asks. One asked about exercise form ("does this dumbbell row need a bench?"), which is about objects and posture. The agent answered it from one flow sheet, and then fell back to YouTube's own preview thumbnails when the download hit a rate limit. Others ask which claim a TikTok makes and where it comes from, or ask for a transcript to summarise with timestamps. The fix is a script over session transcripts that joins each `see` call to the user's prompt, to the sheets and zooms that followed, and to the final answer, and that labels the fact the answer needed: picture-timing, object or pose, claim text, sound, speech words or source. Cheapest settling observation: run it on the 7 sessions that exist today. If at least half of the needed facts are not picture-timing, the bench's question mix is wrong, and the next sense should come from the largest unmeasured class. Runs: nothing new (an offline script).

### m07.8 Calibrate ASR word confidence before any mark reaches the card
"Robot's memory synced and luck" is on the Tears of Steel card as plain fact, and the agent cannot tell a heard word from a guessed one. Names and numbers matter most in a fact-check. Parakeet TDT and whisper both expose per-token probabilities, which we discard. A `?` on low-confidence names and numbers is worth its tokens only if the probability predicts errors. Cheapest settling observation: Blender ships official subtitles for Tears of Steel. Align them to the card's words and compute AUROC of token probability against word error, then repeat on one Vietnamese reel that has creator-typed captions. Below 0.8, the mark would be noise, so drop it and record the number. This is not a duplicate of roadmap step 4, which tests whether agents obey a mark: it tests whether the mark deserves obeying.

## The provocation I would put to the group
The only real-use record we have says agents ran `see` about seven times, never zoomed, and cut the card short with `head`. So the cheapest new sense may be the ones the card already holds and then deletes or truncates, such as persistent text, the synthetic-voice score and the voice legend. Until a real question has been observed to fail for lack of a sensor, building a new one is a bet, not a fix.
