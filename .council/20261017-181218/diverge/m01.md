## Baseline (the obvious answers)
- Speech rate per line (words per second) printed next to each `say` line.
- A loudness curve on the card or in `look` so the agent "sees" loud and quiet parts.
- Long pauses in speech shown as `[pause 1.8 s]` lines, like the existing silences.

## Assumptions I broke
- "A new sense needs a new model": a radio producer's tools are a meter, a stopwatch and headphones on the room tone. Every idea below is DSP on the 16 kHz PCM already decoded plus the word times ASR already returns, so it adds 0 MB and seconds of CPU per hour of audio. The only new cost is a bench for each one.
- "Prosody means emotion, emotion scored 45%, so prosody is done": the rejected probes mapped pitch and loudness onto emotion labels across speakers. A producer never names the feeling. They hear a change against this speaker's own normal, and against this room's own normal. A change against the speaker's own baseline is a measurement with ground truth in the signal ("the gap before this answer is 4x her median gap"), not an inference. That changes what is measured, so the rejection does not cover it.
- (Reading I chose) "perceive" here means questions a listener answers from how the sound was recorded and cut: the room, the gaps, the level, the pace. It does not mean what the sound is. The tagger and ASR already cover what.

## Ideas
### m01.1 Unconformities: the card marks where speech was cut
Analogy: in geology, an unconformity is a surface where rock layers are missing. You find it because the layers on each side do not match, even when no single layer looks wrong. Ported: a producer hears a jump cut in speech because the room tone under the voice changes level or spectrum at a word boundary, a breath gets cut off, or the gap between words is shorter than any natural gap. Each of these can be measured in the gaps between ASR words: noise-floor level and spectral shape in the last and first 100 ms around each gap, and the gap length against the speaker's own distribution. The fix where the analogy breaks: a seam proves something was removed, but not what. So the card says `seam 01:12.4 (room tone jumps 6 dB; gap 40 ms)` and makes no claim about the missing content. This is for the fact-check and "clip from a 47 min talk" jobs, where the real question is "was this edited between these two sentences?". Judge: splice real speech at random word gaps (with and without matching room tone) and count recall against false alarms on the uncut originals. The tears/podcast samples serve as the uncut set.

### m01.2 Room letters: shot letters for the sound
The picture already gives a repeated place the same shot letter. Forensic audio does the same thing for sound: each recording space leaves a fingerprint in its noise floor spectrum (in the gaps) and its reverb tail (how fast energy decays after a word ends, a rough RT60 or direct-to-reverberant ratio). Cluster those per speech line into room letters, `say [voice A, room 1]`, and the agent can answer questions it cannot answer today. Is the narrator in the scene or laid over it (voice-over is dry, close-mic, and its room never matches the picture's shots)? Were these two clips recorded in the same place? Did the interview answer come from a different session than the question? This is different from the futz tag from the last council: that labels a per-line effect, while this tracks identity and continuity across the whole video. Judge: reels where the VO and sync sound are known, plus a multi-room podcast. Score room-letter agreement against hand labels, as the speaker letters were scored (8/8).

### m01.3 The speaker's own clock: hesitation and pace as deviations, not emotions
Analogy: cardiology reads arrhythmia against the patient's own resting rhythm, and seismology triggers on the short-term/long-term average ratio (STA/LTA) rather than on absolute amplitude. Ported: from word times and per-word RMS, keep each voice's median gap before a turn, median syllables per second and median level. Print only the outliers, as plain measurements: `(answer came after 2.6 s; her usual 0.4 s)`, `(fast: 2x his pace)`, `(louder than his usual by 9 dB)`. That answers "did he hesitate before answering?", "where did she slow down to stress a point?" and "when did he raise his voice?". It never claims a feeling. Vietnamese needs syllables per second (one syllable per word, so it is nearly free) because English word rate does not carry over. Judge: a bench of "hesitated / sped up / raised voice: yes or no at T" questions labelled by a listener. Sanity check: the threshold must fire on fewer than about 1 line in 15 on a calm podcast, or the card bloats.

### m01.4 Step-ons: overlap and interruption from the air-traffic-control rulebook
In ATC, a "step-on" is two transmissions keyed at once, and controllers log it because it is where information gets lost. A producer hears crosstalk the same way. The card prints speaker turns as if they never overlap, but ASR word times from two voices that overlap by 300 ms or more, or one 3 s window with two different voice embeddings, mark it: `02:14.1 B cuts in over A (A stops after 0.6 s)` or `A talks through B`. This answers who interrupted whom, who held the floor, and what A said that ASR may have dropped under B. That last one is an integrity flag: overlapped speech is where ASR loses words, which ties into roadmap #4. Judge: AMI or a podcast with labelled overlaps (AMI has reference overlap annotation). Score precision of the interrupter's letter.

### m01.5 The reaction ledger: which line got the laugh
Sports broadcasters find highlights from crowd noise: the loudness of the audience is the label for the play. Ported: CED already names laughter, applause and cheering. A producer attaches each reaction to the line it answers (the last `say` that ended within 1.5 s before it) and ranks the reactions by length and level. The card gets one line per talk: `reactions: 03:41 "...and that's why we ship on Fridays" (laughter 4.2 s), 12:05 "..." (applause 7 s)`. For the "summarise and link talks with timestamps" job, the lines that got applause are the quotable ones, and an agent cannot find them from text. Judge: stand-up and conference talks with timestamped transcripts. Ask "which line got the biggest laugh?" and grade against a listener.

### m01.6 The hot clock: radio's program log, with SponsorBlock as a free judge
Radio runs on a "hot clock": intro bed, talk, sting, ad, back to talk. Each segment has a different bed, room and pace, so segments can be found by change-points in a few cheap features: bed level in the word gaps, room letter (m01.2), pace (m01.3), and music on or off. Ported to YouTube: a sponsor read is read faster, from a script, often in a different room or with a different bed. It shows up as a block-level change in all of them, and so do intros, outros and recaps. The card says `segments: 00:00-00:24 intro (music bed), 03:12-04:05 likely sponsor read (pace 1.4x, room 2)`. The analogy breaks because a station's clock is fixed and a video's is not, so this is change-point detection and not template matching. The judge already exists and costs nothing: SponsorBlock's public database has crowd-labelled sponsor, intro, outro and self-promo spans for millions of YouTube videos. That bench writes itself, which almost no other new sense here can claim.

## The provocation I would put to the group
The card reports what is in the soundtrack. A radio producer listens for what was done to it (the cut, the room, the gap, the level ride), and those are the editor's fingerprints: you read them with a meter, not a model, and a free judge (splices you make yourself, SponsorBlock) exists for most of them.
