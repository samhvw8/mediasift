## Baseline (the obvious answers)
- Add OCR on screen keyframes and a VLM caption per segment so the text carries what moved.
- Add an audio event tagger (laughter, alarm, music) and write the labels into the storyboard.
- Wrap it in an MCP server / one-command `sense VIDEO` that auto-picks flags and returns a small card.

## Assumptions I broke
- "The sensor's job is to choose which frames to show." Film perception says a still is a position sample with no velocity, no direction and no contact. Choosing better samples cannot restore that. Viewers get motion from apparent-motion cues (trails, blur, arrows, ordered change), so the output must encode motion in the pixels or the text, not only pick moments. (Evidence from memory, not re-checked: Marey chronophotography, Bobick and Davis motion history images, Michotte's launching effect, where perceived cause needs contact plus a delay under roughly 150 ms.)
- "Audio equals transcript." Chion's three modes of listening are semantic (words), causal (what made the sound) and reduced (pitch, timbre, rhythm). A transcript keeps only the first. The brief's gap (alarms, laughter, tone, silence) is the other two modes. Sound is also the finer clock: audio resolves milliseconds, video 33-40 ms, so a click or thud dates a visual event better than the frames do.
- "More frames or smarter frames is the dial." Humans do not watch by dense sampling. Event Segmentation Theory (Zacks) says people chunk activity at prediction-error peaks and remember the boundary states. Potter's RSVP work says scene gist survives about 13 ms per image at low detail. So the dial is two-tier: a very cheap low-res gist of everything, and full detail at event boundaries only. That is also how a person watches at 2x speed.

## Ideas
### m05.1 The cursor is the protagonist (sense screen recordings from an action log, not pixels)
For screen recordings (the user's bug repros and walkthroughs), a human follows the cursor and clicks, and the story is "cursor goes to X, click, UI reacts N ms later". Track the cursor (template match on the 128x72 stream, or a recorder wrapper that also writes an input and window-title log at capture time) and emit action lines: "00:12.4 click at (Save); 0.3 s later modal appears". Cause to effect becomes a lag measured in the same signals framesift already has. Not obvious because it makes the recorder, not the decoder, the sensor, and it replaces frames with a few lines of text for the most common daily-use video type.

### m05.2 Output the video as an accessibility artifact: SDH captions + audio description
Subtitles for the deaf and hard of hearing (SDH: "[door slams]", "[tense music]", "(sarcastic)") and audio description for blind viewers are mature crafts with written rules, and LLMs have read enormous amounts of both. The agent is exactly a deaf-blind-ish viewer. Adopt the format as the storyboard (replacing stat lines), and adopt the AD rule as the token budget: describe the picture only where the sound does not already carry it. A lecture or podcast stretch gets text only; silent stretches and UI changes get images. Spend is inversely proportional to audio redundancy, which the existing bench numbers (49% transcript-only on visual questions, but 8/8 on long speech-heavy videos) already hint at.

### m05.3 Ship motion as a picture: motion history image and slit-scan strip per segment
One extra image per segment, built with numpy from the frames already decoded: a motion history image (recent movement bright, older fading, so direction and path read as a gradient trail), and an x-t slit strip (one pixel column per frame, concatenated left to right, where scrolling is a diagonal, a typed line is a growing stripe and a cut is a vertical edge). A person reads direction from a trail in one glance; a multi-frame grid forces the model to infer it. Whether vision models read these is unproven, which makes it a cheap, falsifiable experiment (see m05.7), not a guess.

### m05.4 Event boundary pairs with a "what changed" difference, instead of mid-segment keyframes
Event Segmentation Theory says an event is defined by the state before and the state after. For each segment emit the last frame of the previous state and the first stable frame of the next, plus a changed-region box or crop (the fix for change blindness, where people and models miss a small change between two views). Cause to effect is then two adjacent pairs, and the "what happened between A and B" question is answered by pair A-end and pair B-start. Keyframe count drops, and every image answers a temporal question.

### m05.5 Audio-visual coincidence detector (synchresis) as the anchor for keyframes and causes
Find audio onsets that land within about 80 ms of a visual change. Film editors cut on these, and viewers bind them as one event (Chion's "added value"). Those moments are the clicks, impacts, notifications and cuts-on-beat, i.e. the cause-and-effect candidates, and they are the best places to spend a full-resolution frame. Pair it with a small audio tagger (YAMNet or similar, CPU) for the causal-listening label, run only on windows around coincidences, not on the whole file. Not obvious because it makes the relation between channels the signal, not each channel on its own.

### m05.6 A constant-size "trailer" first pass: gist strip, pacing curve, sound bar
Spend a fixed ~2k tokens regardless of video length: one image of many tiny tiles (gist works at low detail), with under it a sound bar (speech / music / silence / loud events) and a cut-rate curve (average shot length over time, as film scholars use for pacing). The agent sees the shape of the video and where to look, in a form it can act on, then calls find/look for detail. Tile size is the dial to test: image tokens follow pixels, so at some tile size the gist is still readable and 4x more moments fit. Fixed cost also makes the daily command predictable.

### m05.7 Psychophysics bench: arrow-of-time, shuffle and reverse controls
Cheap proof for any claim above, run per change. (a) Reverse test: show the sheet, ask whether a door opens or closes, a form fills or empties, a window appears or vanishes. (b) Shuffle control: if shuffled tiles score the same as ordered, the output does not carry time. (c) Simultaneity and order questions ("did the sound come before the dialog?"). (d) A silent-clip and a muted-clip control to isolate what the audio layer adds. This closes the benchmark gap the brief names (temporal and non-speech-audio questions were never measured) with pass/fail tests that need no human labels.

## The provocation I would put to the group
Stop asking which frames best represent the video; ask what a hearing, sighted person gets from watching that a pile of stills and a transcript cannot give, namely velocity, causal timing and the sound's cause, and build the cheapest signal for each.
